Chapter 4 · the future

How far is far enough?

Moon bases are coming, and so are landers bigger than Apollo's, landing again and again. Flight control needs an answer: where can they touch down without sandblasting everything around them?

There's no single answer. We estimate where the sandblasting falls below a level we picked, using Surveyor 3 as the only real measurement. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a safety rule.

Protection

Pads, berms and craters

A landing pad is a hard, flat surface, like a helipad, so the engine finds no loose dust right under it to blow away. A berm is a ring wall of piled-up soil around the landing spot (50 m out in our model) that catches dust flying low, the way a fence stops a ball rolling along the ground.

Heritage

Left behind on the Moon

Surveyor 3 is not alone. Hardware from every Apollo landing is still up there, and future landings near these sites need the same care.

NASA's own guideline. In 2011 NASA asked anyone landing near these sites to touch down at least 2 km from Apollo and Surveyor landers, or farther if their 3σ landing uncertainty is larger, and to approach along a tangent to that circle rather than straight at it. The reasoning: the lunar horizon is roughly 1.8 km away, so from 2 km the main sheet of fast dust flies over the hardware. These are recommendations, not legal requirements.

Facts from NASA Science mission pages, NASA's NSSDCA catalogue and NASA's 2011 recommendations for lunar heritage sites.

What we learned

Final report

Engineers have studied this since Apollo 12 brought pieces of Surveyor 3 home. What's still open is how it scales: the next landers are far heavier than Apollo's, and the only hard data we have are the parts brought back in 1969.
