Chapter 2 · November 1969

Apollo 12 drops by

Mission goal: land a crewed spacecraft right next to another one. Target: Surveyor 3. Precision landing: GO. Sandblasting the target: not in the flight plan.

Station 1 of 3

The landing

Your job: read how Apollo 12 came down right next to Surveyor 3, in four steps.

1
14 Nov 1969

Launch into a storm

Apollo 12 launches into rain clouds. Lightning hits the rocket twice in the first minute, and the crew flies on.

2
19 Nov 1969, 06:54 UT

A pinpoint landing

Pete Conrad steers the lander Intrepid down just 155 m from Surveyor 3. It proves astronauts can land exactly where they plan.

3
The last metres

A sandblaster

The engine blasts the ground. Dust shoots out sideways, only a few degrees above the surface, and the finest grains fly faster than a rifle bullet. Some of it heads straight for Surveyor.

4
20 Nov 1969

Pieces of Surveyor go home

On their second moonwalk Conrad and Alan Bean walk over, cut off the camera, the scoop and some tubing, and bring about 10 kg of Surveyor parts back to Earth.

Station 3 of 3

The evidence

Your job: look at what the dust did to Surveyor's metal and paint. These pits are the one real measurement behind our model: they set the scale of every number on the map.

What scientists found back on Earth
103 pits per cm²

On metal from the side that faced the lander, scientists counted about 103 tiny pits in every square centimetre, each about as wide as a hair (around 80 µm). The square on the left is 1 cm × 1 cm, drawn with that many pits.

The side facing away was much cleaner: Surveyor 3 had been sandblasted by Apollo 12's engine.

How far away was Surveyor 3? Reports say 155 m (NASA SP-284) or "about 180 m". The LRO-based coordinates in NASA's NSSDCA catalogue (lander 3.0128° S, 23.4219° W; Surveyor 3 3.0162° S, 336.582° E) give 157 m, so our model uses 155 m.

More from the catalogue: Intrepid's descent stage is 4.2 m across, its footpads span 9.4 m, and its engine could throttle up to about 45 kN. After the crew left, the empty ascent stage was crashed into the Moon on purpose, and the seismometer they had just set up recorded the first artificial moonquake.

Photogrammetry of the Apollo landing films puts the dust ejection angle at 1.4° to 8.1° depending on the landing, most of them between 1.4° and 3.3° (Immer, Lane, Metzger, Clements). Pit shapes on Surveyor suggest impact speeds of a few hundred to about 2000 m/s (Brownlee et al. 1972).
