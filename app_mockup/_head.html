Houston, we have a dust problem.

We're two students who like maths. We picked a problem that hasn't mattered much for planning yet: once people live on the Moon, a landing next to their base must not go wrong. So we rebuilt the 1969 dust storm with millions of virtual grains to work out how far is far enough.

Nerd mode: what Surveyor 3 measured

The surface sampler (the scoop on its folding arm) could dig, scrape and press on the soil. From how hard its motor had to push, engineers estimated how strong the ground is: loose and fluffy on top, much firmer a few centimetres down. That told NASA an Apollo lander would not sink.

Surveyor 3's own landing was an accident with a bonus: highly reflective rocks confused its descent radar, so the engine did not cut off at 4.3 m. It bounced to 10 m, then to 3 m, and the footpad prints showed how the soil compresses.

In numbers: the scoop reached 1.5 m from the lander and dug up to 18 cm deep. Surveyor 3 dug four trenches and made four bearing tests and thirteen impact tests. Result: the soil behaves like wet sand and holds about 0.7 kg/cm² (98 kPa), enough for an Apollo lunar module. Launch mass 998 kg. Its TV camera had 25 mm and 100 mm lenses under a movable mirror and needed about 20 seconds per 200-line picture.

Sources: NASA Science, "Surveyor 3" mission page; NASA NSSDC photo catalogue (AS12-H-48-7100, AS12-H-48-7121); NASA SP-284 "Analysis of Surveyor 3 material and photographs returned by Apollo 12" (1972).

The evidence

Your job: look at what the dust did to Surveyor's metal and paint. These pits are the one real measurement our model is checked against.

What scientists found back on Earth
103 pits per cm²

On metal from the side that faced the lander, scientists counted about 103 tiny pits in every square centimetre, each about as wide as a hair (around 80 µm). The square on the left is 1 cm × 1 cm, drawn with that many pits.

The side facing away was much cleaner: Surveyor 3 had been sandblasted by Apollo 12's engine.

How far away was Surveyor 3? Reports say 155 m (NASA SP-284) or "about 180 m". The LRO-based coordinates in NASA's NSSDCA catalogue (lander 3.0128° S, 23.4219° W; Surveyor 3 3.0162° S, 336.582° E) give 157 m, so our model uses 155 m.

More from the catalogue: Intrepid's descent stage is 4.2 m across, its footpads span 9.4 m, and its engine could throttle up to about 45 kN. After the crew left, the empty ascent stage was crashed into the Moon on purpose, and the seismometer they had just set up recorded the first artificial moonquake.

Photogrammetry of the Apollo landing films puts the dust ejection angle at about 1–3° (Immer, Lane, Metzger, Clements). Pit shapes on Surveyor suggest impact speeds of a few hundred to about 2000 m/s (Brownlee et al. 1972).

Chapter 3 · the model

Rebuilding the dust storm

Flying a lander to the Moon to test this is slightly over our budget. So we throw 10 million virtual dust grains instead and count where they land. It works like finding out how often a dart hits the bullseye: throw thousands of darts and count. The method is named Monte Carlo, after the famous casino, because it runs on chance. So are we.

Nerd mode: what this mini model does

Grain sizes follow a log-normal distribution (1–500 µm). Speed comes from Lane & Metzger's fit u ≈ 2054 / √D[µm] m/s, with a random spread for different launch points. Launch angle: one of the six Apollo film angles ± 0.5°. Flight: ballistic in lunar gravity with Moon curvature. A hit means the grain passes the target distance less than 1.5 m above the ground, so it would strike hardware 1.5 m tall.

estimate p = hits / N, uncertainty = 1.96 · √(p(1 − p) / N)

It is a simplified teaching version: the real model also computes how much dust each part of the ground releases at each moment of the descent.

Chapter 4 · the future

How far is far enough?

Moon bases are coming, and so are landers bigger than Apollo's, landing again and again. Flight control needs an answer: where can they touch down without sandblasting everything around them?

There's no single answer. We estimate where the sandblasting falls below a level we picked, using Surveyor 3 as the only real measurement. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a safety rule.

Pads, berms and craters

A landing pad is a hard, flat surface, like a helipad, so the engine finds no loose dust right under it to blow away. A berm is a ring wall of piled-up soil around the landing spot (50 m out in our model) that catches dust flying low, the way a fence stops a ball rolling along the ground.

Nerd mode: how the score works

Your touchdown point sets the distance d to Surveyor 3. The game looks up the model's exposure at d for your lander class (median of the variants that match Surveyor's pits, evaluated 3 m below the plain like Surveyor) and divides it by the 103 pits/cm² of 1969.

It uses the standard descent of the model (34 m down to 2 m at 0.5 m/s), not the exact path you flew.

Final report

Engineers have studied this since Apollo 12 brought Surveyor's camera home. What's still open is how it scales: the next landers are far heavier than Apollo's, and the only hard data point we have is one camera from 1969.
